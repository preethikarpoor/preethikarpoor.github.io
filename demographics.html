Planet
Demographics

Zooming all the way out — asking what the full population of exoplanets looks like, and what that tells us about planet formation.

The Big Picture

Finding individual planets is exciting. But the really profound questions are statistical: how common are planets of different sizes? Do they depend on the type of star they orbit? Is there something special about Earth's size and location, or are rocky planets in habitable zones ordinary?

Exoplanet demographics tries to answer these questions by treating planets as a population — measuring occurrence rates, looking for patterns, and building the statistical picture of what planetary systems look like across the galaxy. It requires large, well-characterized surveys and careful accounting of detection biases.

One of the most striking features in exoplanet demographics is the radius gap — a relative dearth of planets with radii between about 1.5 and 2 Earth radii. It's thought to be caused by photoevaporation: intense stellar radiation stripping the atmospheres off close-in planets. I study this gap and its evolution in M dwarf systems, where the story gets more complex because these stars are far more active than the Sun.

Primary Project · UC San Diego / TESS (2022–Present)
Earth+super-Earth occurrence rates in mid-to-late M dwarfs

My flagship demographic study: using a magnitude-limited sample of mid-to-late M dwarfs observed by TESS to derive occurrence rates for Earth and super-Earth sized planets. The goal is to measure how common small planets are around the most common type of star — with improved completeness corrections and bias accounting compared to previous work.

This work is nearly complete and in preparation for submission. It represents the culmination of the EXOSCAPER pipeline work and multiple observing seasons of TESS data, combined with careful injection-recovery tests to characterize detection efficiency across the sample.
