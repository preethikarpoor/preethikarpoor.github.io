Transit

Finding planets by watching stars blink — and building the pipelines that do it at scale.

The Method

When a planet passes in front of its host star from our point of view, it blocks a tiny fraction of the starlight — causing a measurable dip in brightness. We call this a transit. The dip is small (often less than 1% for Earth-sized planets), brief (hours), and periodic. But with a precise enough telescope and a good enough pipeline, you can find planets this way around thousands of stars simultaneously.

That's exactly what TESS — NASA's Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite — does. It's been watching the sky in 27-day chunks since 2018, and it's found thousands of planet candidates. The challenge isn't collecting the data anymore. It's making sense of it.

That's where my work comes in. I build and use pipelines that take raw TESS light curves and extract reliable planet detections from them — accounting for stellar noise, instrument systematics, and the inevitable false positives that look like planets but aren't.

Project · UC San Diego (2022–2024)
EXOSCAPER — Exoplanet Survey and Characterization Program for Earth-like Rocky Planets

EXOSCAPER is my primary research project and the thing I'm most proud of from my time at UCSD. It's a scalable, modular, end-to-end pipeline for detecting and validating exoplanet candidates in TESS photometry — built specifically to handle the messy reality of M dwarf light curves.

M dwarfs are extraordinarily active stars. They flare. They spot. Their variability can easily mimic the signal of a transiting planet — or bury a real signal entirely. EXOSCAPER addresses this by integrating noise modeling, flare detection and mitigation, multi-aperture photometry extraction, transit search (BLS and TLS), and an automated vetting framework into a single, reproducible pipeline.

The science goal: derive occurrence rates for Earth and super-Earth sized planets around mid-to-late M dwarfs. This is the demographic picture of small planets around the most common type of star in the galaxy. The pipeline is the means; the statistics are the point.

Project · California Institute of Technology (2021–2022)
Teaching machines to find transits — Transformer models for TESS

Before EXOSCAPER, I was at Caltech working with Dr. Ashish Mahabal on a fundamentally different approach to the same problem: can deep learning find transiting planets that classical methods miss?

I designed and trained Transformer-based models — both Time-Series Transformers operating directly on flux sequences, and Vision Transformers processing phase-folded light curves as images. These architectures, now famous for powering large language models, turned out to be surprisingly well-suited to learning the subtle morphology of transit signals buried in stellar noise.

The result: detections with higher sensitivity to shallow signals from small planets, especially around variable stars where traditional methods struggle.
