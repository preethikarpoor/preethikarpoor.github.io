Direct
Imaging

Blocking out a star's glare to photograph the planets orbiting it — and figuring out what their atmospheres are made of.

The Method

Most of the planets we know about were found indirectly — through their gravitational tug on their star, or the shadows they cast during transits. Direct imaging is different: it's a photograph. We point a telescope at a star, block out its blinding light with a coronagraph, and look for the faint glow of reflected starlight (or thermal emission) from a planet orbiting beside it.

It's extraordinarily hard. A star outshines its planets by a factor of a billion or more at optical wavelengths. The planet's light is buried in diffracted starlight, telescope vibrations, and atmospheric turbulence. But it's also uniquely powerful — because once you've imaged a planet, you can take its spectrum. You can read its atmosphere.

That's what my work at IIA Bengaluru is building toward. I'm focused on SCALES — a new integral-field spectrograph being built for the Keck Observatory — which will be one of the most powerful tools ever built for directly imaging and spectroscopically characterizing young, wide-separation giant planets.

Project · Indian Institute of Astrophysics, Bengaluru (2025–Present)
SCALES — Santa Cruz Array of Lenslets for Exoplanet Spectroscopy

SCALES is an upcoming thermal-infrared integral-field spectrograph for the Keck telescope, designed specifically to characterize the atmospheres of directly imaged planetary companions. It operates in a wavelength regime that lets it see through the infrared window of planetary atmospheres — detecting signatures of water, methane, carbon monoxide, and other molecules that tell us about temperature, composition, and dynamics.

My role at IIA is on the science and pipeline side: I'm conducting feasibility studies for atmospheric modeling and retrieval using SCALES, simulating what the instrument will actually observe for different planetary atmosphere models, and developing the observational strategy and pipeline infrastructure needed to make those observations scientifically productive.

This is pre-first-light science development — the kind of groundwork that makes it possible to hit the ground running when a new instrument comes online. It involves working at the intersection of instrument modeling, atmospheric physics, and spectral data reduction.
