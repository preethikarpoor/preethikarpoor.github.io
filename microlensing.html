Using gravity as a lens — and building the pipelines that will let Roman see thousands of these fleeting events.

The Method

Gravitational microlensing is one of the most elegant ideas in all of astronomy. When a foreground star passes almost exactly in front of a background star from our point of view, the foreground star acts as a gravitational lens — bending and amplifying the background star's light. The result is a characteristic brightening event, peaking when the alignment is closest, then fading as the stars drift apart.

If the foreground lens star has a planet, that planet creates its own tiny lensing perturbation — a brief anomaly in the light curve that can last anywhere from hours to days. It's fleeting, unrepeatable, and often only a few percent above the baseline. But it's also one of the only ways to detect cold planets at wide orbital separations — worlds that transit surveys and radial velocity methods can't reach.

The Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope's Galactic Bulge Time Domain Survey is going to be the most ambitious microlensing survey ever conducted — staring at hundreds of millions of stars simultaneously. The question is: how sensitive will it actually be? That's what I'm working to quantify.

Project · Louisiana State University (2025–Present)
Roman Space Telescope — Photometry Precision Pipeline

Working with Prof. Matthew Penny at LSU, I'm building an end-to-end photometry precision pipeline for Roman's Wide Field Instrument — the 300-megapixel camera that will execute the Galactic Bulge survey. The pipeline benchmarks achievable photometric precision across different source extraction methods (PSF fitting, aperture photometry, difference imaging) and observing cadences.

The output is a quantitative noise and error budget: a precise statement of what Roman will be able to detect, at what sensitivity, for what range of planetary masses and separations. This feeds directly into exoplanet yield forecasts — predictions of how many planets of each type Roman will find over its mission lifetime.

It also addresses microlensing event detectability more specifically: given a particular planet-star configuration producing a particular perturbation shape, can Roman's photometric precision recover it? The answer depends critically on getting the noise model right.
